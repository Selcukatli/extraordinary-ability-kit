# Petition Narrative

Drafting the petition letter — the document that tells the adjudicator what to conclude
and why.

> Draft it, then have an immigration attorney revise and file it. This skill produces a
> complete, well-argued draft; it does not replace counsel.

## Structure

A petition letter runs 15–30 pages:

1. **Introduction** — who the beneficiary is, the field of endeavor stated narrowly, which
   criteria are claimed, and the conclusion you want reached
2. **Background** — career arc and standing in the field
3. **Step 1 — criterion by criterion.** One section per claimed criterion: quote the
   regulatory language, state the evidence, cite the exhibits, explain why it satisfies
   the criterion
4. **Step 2 — final merits determination.** The totality argument
5. **Conclusion** — will continue working in the area; will substantially benefit the U.S.

Load the criteria manifest from `petition-strategy` for the correct list and citations —
O-1A has 8 criteria, EB-1A has 10, and they are not interchangeable.

## Where petitions are actually won and lost

**Step 1 is a checkbox exercise. Step 2 is where denials happen.** A petition that
carefully proves four criteria and then treats the final merits determination as a
one-paragraph formality has skipped the part the adjudicator weighs hardest.

Section 4 should argue a *narrative*, not re-list the checkboxes: this person defined a
problem, solved it in a way others adopted, and the field moved. Recognition, publication,
judging invitations, and compensation are all downstream evidence of that one story.

## Rules for the argument

- **Name the field narrowly and use the same phrase throughout.** Drift between
  "computer vision," "robotics," and "AI" invites the adjudicator to pick the broadest
  reading and find the applicant unremarkable within it.
- **Every claim cites an exhibit.** An assertion with no exhibit number is invisible.
- **Give every metric a comparison.** "900 citations" means nothing alone; "900 citations
  where the field median for comparable work is under 100" is an argument.
- **Explain significance, never assert it.** "Major significance" is a conclusion the
  adjudicator must reach, not a phrase that establishes anything by being written down.
- **Preempt the obvious weakness.** If a criterion is thin, address it directly rather
  than hoping it passes. Unaddressed weaknesses become RFEs.
- **Quote recommenders sparingly and specifically.** A short quote that states a concrete
  fact beats a long one that praises.

## Tone

Formal, declarative, specific. Cite dates, named organizations, and numbers. Do not
oversell — an adjudicator who catches one inflated claim discounts the rest of the
petition. Where evidence is genuinely strong, plain statement is more persuasive than
adjectives.
